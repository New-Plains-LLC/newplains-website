# How to Calculate the ROI of an AI Automation Project

To calculate the return on an AI automation project, estimate the realistic annual value of the work it can recover or improve, subtract the full implementation and operating costs, and compare the result with the investment. The difficult part is not the formula. It is choosing honest assumptions.

## Start with a baseline

Measure the workflow before changing it. At minimum, record:

- How often the workflow runs.
- How many people touch it.
- How many total hours it consumes.
- The fully loaded cost of that time.
- How long customers or employees wait.
- How often the work is delayed, incomplete, or corrected.
- What work does not happen because the team lacks capacity.

Without a baseline, the company may launch an impressive system without knowing whether it improved the business.

## Calculate current labor cost

The simplest annual calculation is:

**Weekly hours × fully loaded hourly cost × 52**

If a workflow consumes 20 hours per week and the fully loaded labor cost is $30 per hour, the annual labor cost is:

**20 × $30 × 52 = $31,200**

Fully loaded cost may include wages, payroll taxes, benefits, and other direct employment costs. Use the company’s actual finance assumptions rather than a generic multiplier.

## Estimate the recoverable share

Do not assume the automation eliminates 100% of the work. People will still review exceptions, correct bad inputs, handle unusual situations, and maintain the process.

If the business believes 50% of those hours can be recovered, the modeled annual labor value is:

**$31,200 × 50% = $15,600**

That $15,600 is capacity value. It becomes cash savings only if the company actually reduces a cash expense. Often the benefit appears as more work completed, quicker responses, less overtime, or employees spending more time on customers and decisions.

## Include the full cost

The investment side should include:

| Cost | Examples |
|---|---|
| Discovery | Workflow audit, process mapping, feasibility assessment |
| Implementation | Integration, configuration, prompts, testing, documentation |
| Software | Automation platforms, databases, phone, messaging, hosting |
| AI usage | Model, voice, image, document, or search usage |
| Internal time | Employee input, testing, training, and review |
| Ongoing management | Monitoring, support, updates, and improvements |

For a one-year model:

**Year-one cost = one-time costs + 12 months of recurring costs**

## Calculate three useful numbers

### Year-one net value

**Annual benefit − year-one cost**

Using the $15,600 modeled labor value, a $6,000 implementation, and $250 per month in ongoing costs:

**$15,600 − $6,000 − ($250 × 12) = $6,600**

### Return on investment

**(Annual benefit − year-one cost) ÷ year-one cost × 100**

In this example:

**$6,600 ÷ $9,000 × 100 = 73% modeled year-one ROI**

### Payback period

**Upfront investment ÷ monthly net benefit**

Monthly gross labor value is $1,300. After the $250 recurring cost, monthly net benefit is $1,050.

**$6,000 ÷ $1,050 = approximately 5.7 months**

These calculations assume the full benefit begins immediately. Real projects usually ramp up, so add a conservative adoption period.

## Model more than labor

Labor is easiest to quantify, but it is not the only source of value.

### Response time

Faster lead response or customer follow-up may improve conversion or retention. Use company-specific history if available. Do not insert a broad internet benchmark and treat it as your result.

### Capacity

An automation may allow the current team to process more invoices, customers, service requests, reports, or locations without another hire.

### Error and rework reduction

Measure how often records are corrected, duplicated, missed, or sent back. Estimate the time and direct cost of that rework.

### Decision speed

Recurring reports and summaries may give managers usable information earlier. The value is real, but it should be described carefully unless the business can connect faster information to a measurable result.

### Risk reduction

Logs, consistent routing, required fields, and escalation rules can reduce operational risk. Avoid assigning a dollar value unless the company has a defensible method.

## Run three scenarios

Build a conservative, expected, and upside case.

| Assumption | Conservative | Expected | Upside |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Time recovered | 25% | 50% | 70% |
| Adoption speed | Slow | Moderate | Fast |
| Exceptions | High | Normal | Low |
| Ongoing cost | Higher | Expected | Lower |

If the project only makes sense in the upside case, it is probably not ready.

## Measure after launch

Compare the same measures used in the baseline:

- Hours spent per completed workflow.
- Volume completed.
- Average response or cycle time.
- Exceptions and corrections.
- Human-review rate.
- Software and model usage cost.
- System failures and time to recovery.

Review after 30, 60, and 90 days. An automation may need prompt changes, better source data, tighter rules, or a redesigned handoff before it reaches the expected value.

## The honest business case

Automation ROI is not “hours multiplied by salary” with every hour treated as cash. It is the measurable value the company actually captures after implementation, adoption, exceptions, and ongoing costs.
