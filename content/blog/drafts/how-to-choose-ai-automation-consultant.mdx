# How to Choose an AI Automation Consultant: 12 Questions to Ask

Choose an AI automation consultant who can define the workflow, explain the business case, build and test the system, identify failure modes, and state who owns and maintains the result. Avoid choosing on demos, model names, or broad promises of transformation.

The best evaluation starts with one real workflow from your business. Ask each consultant how they would approach it and listen for operational detail.

## 1. What workflow would you recommend we start with, and why?

A serious consultant should ask about frequency, labor, delays, errors, systems, risk, and value before recommending a project.

A weak answer begins with a product. A strong answer begins with the work.

## 2. What will you deliver before implementation begins?

Discovery should produce something concrete: current-state findings, proposed workflow, architecture, assumptions, risks, success measures, and a build estimate.

Ask whether you own that work and whether you can use it with another provider.

## 3. Who will actually build the system?

Determine whether the people selling the project are also responsible for architecture and implementation. If subcontractors or offshore teams are involved, understand who reviews their work and who has access to company systems.

## 4. How will the automation connect to our existing tools?

The provider should identify supported integrations, APIs, export methods, authentication, and any manual bridge that will remain.

Be cautious if the proposal assumes every system connects cleanly without verification.

## 5. Where will human review remain?

Ask what the system can complete automatically, what requires approval, and what conditions trigger escalation.

The answer should reflect the consequence of mistakes. Drafting an internal summary and issuing a customer refund should not have the same controls.

## 6. How will you test the system?

Testing should use real, sanitized examples including normal cases, incomplete inputs, duplicates, conflicting information, and unusual exceptions.

Ask for acceptance criteria. “The agent seems to work” is not an acceptance test.

## 7. What happens when a connected system or credential fails?

Production systems need logs, error handling, alerts, retries, and an owner. The consultant should explain how the business will know when the automation stops, produces incomplete work, or encounters a case it cannot handle.

## 8. How do you control data and system access?

Ask about least-privilege access, credential storage, environments, data retention, model-provider policies, logging, backups, and offboarding.

The provider should be able to explain security in plain language without pretending risk can be eliminated.

## 9. What will the project cost after launch?

Request a list of continuing expenses:

- Software subscriptions.
- Model and usage charges.
- Hosting and storage.
- Phone, email, or messaging fees.
- Monitoring and support.
- Future changes.

An inexpensive build can carry a costly operating model.

## 10. Who owns the finished system?

Clarify ownership of source code, workflow configuration, prompts, credentials, generated data, indexes, documentation, and vendor accounts.

Also ask whether another provider can maintain the system later.

## 11. What support is included?

Define the support period, response expectations, monitoring responsibilities, and what counts as a defect versus a new request.

For ongoing engagements, ask how priorities are chosen and how completed work is documented.

## 12. How will we measure the result?

The consultant should establish a baseline and agree on a few practical measures, such as hours per completed workflow, response time, volume, exception rate, rework, adoption, and operating cost.

Avoid guaranteed ROI claims based solely on estimated labor hours.

## Compare providers with a scorecard

| Category | Weight | What a strong answer includes |
|---|---:|---|
| Workflow understanding | 20% | Specific steps, bottlenecks, exceptions, owner |
| Implementation ability | 20% | Integrations, testing, deployment, documentation |
| Business case | 15% | Baseline, assumptions, full cost, measurable result |
| Security and controls | 15% | Permissions, credentials, logs, human review |
| Ownership and portability | 10% | Client access to source, data, configuration |
| Support model | 10% | Clear post-launch responsibilities |
| Communication and training | 10% | Plain explanations and employee adoption plan |

Score the evidence, not the presentation.

## Red flags

- Recommending a platform before understanding the workflow.
- Promising to automate the entire company at once.
- Treating a prototype as production-ready.
- Avoiding questions about security or failure handling.
- Hiding recurring costs.
- Refusing to define ownership.
- Using impressive demos that do not involve your systems or data.
- Quoting savings without a baseline.
